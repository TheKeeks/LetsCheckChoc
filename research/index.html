How the energy is counted
  • A crest of rays 24 km wide, 100 m apart, leaves with your heading.
  • Along the way: rays within a wavelength-wide window of your wave (about 1.1 km at 8 s, 1.5 km at 12 s, 1.8 km at 16 s), since real waves smear out over about that width.
  • At the shore: the count again for directions out to ±10° in 2.5° steps, weighted like the main model's spread, each on the part of the swell headed for the spot.
  • Likely range: the wider of the ray-counting noise and the change between neighboring directions.
  • Height adds shallowing, read at 4 m of water.
  • Ray tracing as in "How does the model work?": crest speed from depth and period, turning toward slower water (Snell's law).
  • Deep water: NOAA ETOPO 2022 (about 1 km, smoothed over 1.2 km, 400 m steps on the curved Earth). Survey map: 64 m, smoothed over 150 m, 32 m steps, traced live.
  • Wave speed at each depth from the exact wave equation, checked against Snell's law on a test beach. Times at group speed, how fast wave energy travels. No bottom friction or breaking.

The method

How does the model work?

Swell arrives from the open ocean, and the sea floor bends it on the way in, the way a lens bends light: waves slow down over shallow water and turn toward it. Islands and points block it. So only some of the swell the buoy measures ever reaches a given beach, and which part depends on the swell's direction and period.

How size and shape are worked out

  1. Send the swell in. A line of rays, 20 m apart, enters wherever the swell crosses the open-ocean edge of the map, square to the swell. Each ray carries the same share of energy.
  2. Let the sea floor bend them. This is linear wave ray tracing (geometric optics, the standard refraction method in coastal engineering). Two rules:
    • Speed from depth. A crest's speed c at each point comes from the swell's period T and the local depth h through the linear dispersion relation, solved exactly at every depth. That is about 1.56·T m/s in deep water, slowing toward √(g·h) in the shallows. The deeper the water a wave can feel, the longer its period: 6 s swell starts slowing in about 28 m, 15 s swell in about 175 m.
    • Turn toward slower water. This is Snell's law applied continuously. Over each step ds the ray turns by dθ = −(1/c)·(∂c/∂n)·ds, where ∂c/∂n is how fast the speed changes across the ray. When one end of a crest is in shallower, slower water, the crest swings toward it.
    The depth is NOAA's survey (CUDEM), averaged to 144 m cells and smoothed over about 350 m, so the rays follow the shape of the bottom on the scale of a wavelength rather than single survey pixels. The step is 72 m. Rays that hit land stop. Left out: diffraction (energy spreading sideways into shadows, allowed for roughly by the wavelength-wide landing zones below), currents, wind and bottom friction.
  3. Count who lands. Count the rays that pass through each spot's zone and compare with open water, where a patch that size would be crossed by a fixed number. More rays than that means the bottom funnels energy in; fewer means it spreads it out or blocks it. Waves also build a little as they slow down; both spots are measured at the same depth (4 m), so neither gets a boost from shallower water. The square root of all that is the share of the buoy's height that arrives: size.
  4. Check the angle they arrive at. For every ray that lands, we measure the angle between it and the bottom contours where it gets there, from the 3–8 m survey. Square-on (under 30°) means the whole wave breaks at once and closes out; at an angle, it breaks progressively along the contours and peels. The share arriving at 30° or more is the shape. Surf scientists call this the peel angle. It says whether a wave can peel, not how fast or how good.

For real days the same counts are applied to every slice of the buoy's measured spectrum (each period with its own direction) and added up, giving a modeled height at each spot for every hour since 2016.

Question 2

How real is the swell window?

From Choc, the straight-line gap to open ocean runs from Block Island (115°) to Montauk Point (158°). Swell from inside it has a straight shot at the reef, and that is still where Choc does best. But the sea floor bends swell around both islands, so the window's edges are soft, and most of Choc's 3 ft+ days get their swell from outside it.

54%of Choc's modeled energy during your 29 Aug 2023 sessions ("chest high", "rippable Cardiff vibes", size 6) came from outside the 115–158° window. The buoy had one swell, 5.4 ft at 11 s from 165°: past Montauk, where there is no straight path to the reef, so more than half of what reached Choc bent around the point.
Question 2 · swell trains

One swell or two?

The buoy often feels more than one swell at once: a groundswell from a storm far away plus waves from the local wind, coming from different directions. A "swell train" is one of those. The model already splits the buoy's reading by period and direction, so it counts each train separately. Here are the trains it finds.

30%of days have two or more trains of 1 ft+ at the buoy when Choc is at its biggest.
1 trainmakes the surf on almost every 3 ft+ day at Choc. On a typical big day the biggest train at the buoy brings all of Choc's swell energy; even on the most mixed 10% of days it brings 98%.
20 daysin ten years (3 ft+ at Choc) where the buoy's biggest train wasn't the one making the surf. Usually a short north-to-east wind sea sat on top of a longer SE swell. Going by the buoy's single main direction, those days look blocked.

The clearest case: 4 January 2018, the bomb cyclone. The buoy read 13.8 ft at 7 s from the NNE, which barely reaches Choc (2.2 ft). Underneath was 12.8 ft at 10.5 s from the ESE (121°), which reached Choc at 7.1 ft. Pick that date below to see it.

How far ahead can you trust the forecast?

Exploratory · a second spot

Wilderness: the east-swell, east-wind left

Wilderness is a left point break on Fishers' south shore, about 4 km west of Choc, along 41.25286° N 72.00573° W to 41.25551° N 72.01117° W. The shore faces SSW, and the point at its east end shelters it from east wind. Its zone is the 150 m strip along that shoreline, and the same forward rays as Choc are counted there (How the model works shows both).

  • Size: it gets less swell than Choc. It faces SSW and sits behind the curve of Fishers, so it's more sheltered. Over ten years Wilderness models 2.5 ft+ on 698 days against Choc's 1,012, and only beats Choc by half a foot or more on 9 days. It does relatively best on east swell (about Choc's size when the buoy's swell comes from 0–120°) and worst on south to south-west swell (about 0.75× Choc).
  • Wind: this is why Wilderness days exist. The point at its east end blocks east and north-east wind, which blows Choc out. "Wilderness days" (3 hours with Wilderness 2.5 ft+ and wind it likes, while Choc's wind is not clean) happened on 300 days in ten years, about 30 a year, mostly September–October and February–April.
  • Shape: what decides whether you go, and the weakest part of the model. You surf it when it peels, and it peels best when long-period swell wraps the point. The model's shape read (the share of swell meeting the bottom contours at 30°+, the "peel angle") agrees that east swell peels better than south-east. But it says longer periods peel less, the opposite of what you see, and it barely varies from day to day (almost all days score 0.42–0.55). Most likely it measures the angle too far out (within 400 m, on 48 m cells) to see the wrap around the point. So treat the shape column below as experimental, not as a reason to go or not.

How much to trust it: less than Choc. A point break works by wrapping swell around the point and peeling down the shore, which is mostly diffraction plus refraction over the last few hundred meters. On 48 m cells the rays see the shape of the point, but not the wave breaking or wrapping in the last few dozen meters. So far there is one Wilderness session to check it against, below. One good day shows the model isn't wrong about that day; it can't validate the model. Logging a handful more Wilderness sessions, good and flat, would let us test it the way Choc was tested.

Peak reading or a sustained window?

Each candidate daily measure was computed from the buoy for every logged session, using all hours, then ranked against the size you rated. The whiskers are 95% bootstrap ranges.

  • Peak and sustained are a tie. The best 3 h through a soft window scores 0.65, the in-window peak 0.67, and the any-direction peak 0.63. Every whisker overlaps, and the 3 h and 6 h windows are indistinguishable.
  • The window edge matters more than the averaging. A hard 115–158° cut scores your Ian 10/10 (163° at the buoy) as nearly flat. The soft edge beat the hard cut in 93% of resamples.
  • So the page uses the best 3 hours through a soft window. Three hours is about one session or one tide push, and one spiky hour can't make a day. The map's Past view still shows the buoy's peak, because that's how people talk about swell.

How much the data sources disagree

Every model tracks the buoy's ups and downs well. They part ways on how big the peak is and on whether swell is inside the window: the window is 43° wide and the models sit 12–16° off the buoy's direction on a typical hour.

Size × period is wave power
Measuring "big"

Size × period is wave power

power ≈ 0.49 × height² × period  →  kW per meter of wave crest

Double the height and you get 4× the power. Double the period and you get 2×. Your two 10/10 days, Larry (6.4 ft @ 15.4 s) and Ian (8.8 ft @ 8.1 s), land within 1% of each other on height² × period. Every energy number on this page is that power, counted only for the part of the swell coming through the window.

What's next
Research process

What's next

  1. Keep logging, especially off-season, flat days and Wilderness. The ratings model has 28 Choc sessions, none from November to May, only two flat ones, and no logged Wilderness sessions. Wilderness numbers can't be checked against anything until there are some.
  2. Read the logs live. The ten-year calendar now reads photos from the site's surf log. The ratings model still fits from a CSV export; reading the logs live would let it refit after every new session.
  3. Sharper sea floor for Choc's number. Tracing the last kilometer and a half on the 8 m survey, with your reef sketch as the landing zone, puts rays on the reef itself instead of a 650 m circle. It would replace the ten-year numbers only if it ranks your logged sessions better than the current model.
  4. Sharper wind. The 17-point wind grid uses the ECMWF 9 km model. NOAA's HRRR (3 km, archived since 2018) would resolve the wind over the reef better.
